User handbook

Running your municipality on the GIS platform

Everything a Municipality Owner, Department Head, or Department User needs to log in, set up departments and staff, get real GIS data onto the map, and control who can see what — walked through with the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation setup as a live example.

Who does what
Owner— runs the whole municipality
Dept. Head— approves their department's data
Dept. User— uploads their department's data
01 — Read first

How the platform is organized

One thing to understand before anything else: every municipality is its own sealed world.

  • One account, one municipality. When you log in, you only ever see your own municipality's departments, users, and GIS data — Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation staff can never see Somnath's data, or vice versa, no matter what.
  • Three roles, always exactly three. MUNICIPALITY_OWNER, DEPARTMENT_HEAD, DEPARTMENT_USER. The Owner is created automatically when the municipality registers; everyone else is created by the Owner and assigned to a department.
  • GIS data has a lifecycle. A file doesn't appear on the map the moment it's uploaded — it moves through validate → review → approve → publish first. This is deliberate: it's the same checkpoint a paper survey would go through before being filed as an official record.
  • Two kinds of layers. Canonical layers (municipal boundary, wards, zones) are reference data anyone in the municipality can see. Department layers (a water pipeline network, a road centerline dataset) belong to one department, and are only visible to others if that department — or the Owner — decides to share them.

02 — Getting in
everyone

Log in

Open the app and sign in with the email and password an Owner gave you.

  1. Go to the site.

    Open the app in your browser (during development, http://localhost:4200) and choose Login in the top bar.

  2. Sign in.

    Enter your email and password. This screen is handled by the platform's identity service (Keycloak) — the same login page is shared by every municipality, so there's nothing municipality-specific to pick here.

  3. Land on your dashboard.

    A successful login takes you straight to your Dashboard, already scoped to your municipality and role — no extra selection step.

No account yet? A brand-new municipality is created from the Register Municipality link on the landing page — that flow creates the municipality and its first Owner account together. Every other account (Department Heads and Users) is created afterward by that Owner from the Users page — see Chapter 05.
03 — Home base
everyone

Dashboard

The first thing you see after logging in — a scoped summary of your municipality's GIS data, not a generic landing page.

  • Your identity card. Your name, municipality, role, and department (if any).
  • GIS overview cards. Total Layers, Published Layers, Draft Layers, Departments, and GIS Data Sources — each one is a live count of what you are actually allowed to see, not a raw municipality-wide number. Click any card to jump straight to the map.
  • GIS data by department. One row per department with how many layers you can view in it. Click a row to open the map already filtered and zoomed to that department's data.
Why the numbers can differ per person Two people at the same municipality can see different counts on this exact same dashboard. A Road Department User sees 0 layers under "Water Department" unless the Owner has specifically granted their department cross-access — the dashboard reflects permissions, not just the raw data that exists.
04 — Structure
Owner

Set up departments

Departments are the organizational unit everything else attaches to — GIS layers, users, and permissions. Set these up before creating staff accounts.

  1. Open Departments.

    From the top navigation. Only an Owner can create, rename, or deactivate a department — Heads and Users can view the list but not edit it.

  2. Create a department.

    Give it a name (e.g. Water Department) and a short code (e.g. WATER) — the code is normalized to uppercase automatically and is what appears in generated layer names and internal references.

  3. Repeat for each real department.

    One row per operational unit that will own its own GIS data — in Ahmedabad's case: Road, Water, and Tourism.

Name departments after real units, not data types A department owns whatever data that real-world team is responsible for — "Water Department" ends up owning valves, pipelines, and pumping stations together. Don't create a separate department per dataset.
05 — Staff
Owner

Add staff accounts

Every account below the Owner is created here, assigned to exactly one department and one role.

  1. Open Users, then Add User.

    Fill in name, email, and phone number, then choose a Department and a Role.

  2. Choose the role deliberately.

    DEPARTMENT_HEAD can approve data their department uploads by default. DEPARTMENT_USER can upload but not approve their own work — someone else in the chain has to sign off.

  3. Share the generated password.

    Creating a user generates a one-time temporary password. Pass it to that person through a separate, secure channel — they'll be prompted to set their own password the first time they log in.

06 — Bringing in data
Head / User

Upload GIS data

From GIS Data in the navigation. Accepts a zipped Shapefile, a GeoJSON file, or a CSV with coordinate columns.

  1. Choose the file.

    A .zip containing a complete Shapefile (.shp .shx .dbf .prj), a .geojson, or a .csv.

  2. Describe the layer.

    Give it a name (e.g. "Water Pipeline"), an optional description, and choose its ownership: Department (belongs to your department, the normal case) or Canonical (municipality-wide reference data — Owner only).

  3. CSV only — map the coordinates.

    If your file is a CSV, point the wizard at your latitude/longitude or X/Y columns and confirm the coordinate system they're in. Shapefiles and GeoJSON carry this information already, so this step is skipped for them.

  4. Validation runs automatically.

    The platform inspects the file and reports back the feature count, geometry type, detected coordinate system, and attribute fields — or a clear error if something's wrong (a corrupt archive, a missing .prj, an unreadable column).

  5. Submit for review.

    Once validation succeeds, submit it — this hands the upload to whoever can approve it (see next chapter). It is not on the map yet.

What "validated" actually checks Geometry reads cleanly, the coordinate system is known (or you confirmed it), and the data imports without errors. It does not check whether the content itself is correct — a Water Department User uploading Water Department data is still expected to know what they're uploading.
07 — Sign-off
Head / Owner

Review, approve & publish

A second checkpoint before anything reaches the live map — by default, the person who uploaded a layer is never the one who approves it.

  1. Approve or reject.

    Whoever has APPROVE on that department — a Department Head, by default — reviews the validated upload and either approves it or rejects it with a reason the uploader can see.

  2. Publish.

    An approved upload still isn't live — publishing is a separate, deliberately narrower permission. By default nobody but the Owner has PUBLISH; the Owner can extend it to a Head if that department should self-publish.

  3. Re-uploads replace, they don't duplicate.

    Publishing a file with the same layer name as an existing layer replaces its data as a new version — the layer keeps the same identity on the map and in permissions, it just points at fresher data.

Worked example

Sneha (Roads, User) uploads an updated Footpaths file and submits it for review. Rakesh (Roads, Head) opens GIS Data, reviews the validation summary, and approves it. Because Roads hasn't been granted PUBLISH, the file sits as APPROVED until the Owner publishes it — at which point it appears on the map for everyone with VIEW access.

08 — Access control
Owner

Manage layers & permissions

GIS Layers in the navigation lists every published layer. Open one to reach its permission matrix.

  • Six permissions, per layer, per department: VIEW, UPLOAD, APPROVE, PUBLISH, EXPORT, MANAGE.
  • A department's own layers start with sensible defaults — its Head gets VIEW/UPLOAD/APPROVE, its Users get VIEW/UPLOAD. Nobody gets PUBLISH, EXPORT, or MANAGE until the Owner grants it.
  • Cross-department sharing is one checkbox. To let Road Department view Water's pipeline layer, open the Water Pipeline layer's permission page and grant VIEW to the Roads role — that's the entire mechanism behind Water User seeing 1 Road layer in the earlier dashboard example.
  • Only the Owner reaches this page. Everyone else only sees the layers and permissions that already apply to them.
MANAGE is the permission that grants other permissions Hand it out sparingly — a department with MANAGE on a layer can change who else can view, upload, approve, publish, or export it.
09 — The map
everyone

Use the map viewer

Municipal GIS in the navigation — or arrive here directly by clicking through from the Dashboard.

  • Layer panel, grouped by who owns the data. "Canonical Layers" at the top (boundary, wards, zones), then "Department Layers" broken into its own heading per department — Road, Water, Tourism each get their own list rather than one mixed pile. Only layers you have VIEW access to appear at all.
  • Toggle a layer on to see it and its legend swatch next to its checkbox — off again to hide it, without reloading the map.
  • Search the box above the map for a layer name, road name, or ward name — results are split into matching layers and matching individual features, and both respect the same view permissions as everything else.
  • Click anywhere on the map with a layer turned on to inspect the feature there — its attributes open in a panel, read-only.
  • Standard controls sit in the corner: zoom in/out, reset to the municipality's own extent, fullscreen, a basemap switcher (OpenStreetMap plus two alternates), a scale bar, and live cursor coordinates.
This is a viewer, not an editor Nothing on the map can be drawn, moved, or edited here — correcting a feature means fixing the source file and going through the upload → review → publish cycle again.
